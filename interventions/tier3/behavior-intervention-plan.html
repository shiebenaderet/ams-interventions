Behavior Intervention Plan (BIP)

Rating: ★★★★★ (Legally required when applicable) Tier: 3 (Intensive) Time: Ongoing Group Size: Individual + team

What It Is

A formal, written plan built from a Functional Behavior Assessment (FBA) that identifies the function of a student's behavior (what need it's meeting) and outlines specific strategies to teach replacement behaviors, reinforce positive ones, and respond to problem behaviors consistently.

Legal requirement: Under IDEA, a BIP is required when a student's behavior impedes their own learning or the learning of others — especially for students with IEPs. If a behavior results in a disciplinary removal of 10+ days, an FBA and BIP are legally required. When in doubt, consult with the school psychologist, case manager, or administrator.
Used at AMS by: Social Studies lists "specific behavior contracts" as a Tier 3 practice. PE/Health uses ABC data tracking that feeds directly into FBA/BIP work. The school psychologist leads BIP development.

Quality Implementation

What TO Do

  • Start with an FBA — you can't build a plan without knowing the function
  • Identify a replacement behavior that meets the same need as the problem behavior
  • Teach the replacement behavior explicitly — it will not happen spontaneously
  • Reinforce the replacement behavior consistently, especially early
  • All staff who interact with the student follow the same plan — inconsistency undermines it
  • Data collection built in: what's happening, when, how often, what follows

Red Flags

  • BIP that's all punishment and no instruction — misses the whole point
  • Plan written by one adult, never shared with the rest of the team
  • No data collection — can't tell if it's working
  • Responding to problem behaviors in ways that reinforce them (attention, escape, etc.)
  • Teaching replacement behaviors only in the moment of crisis (too late)

The Four Common Functions of Behavior

FunctionWhat the Student Is GettingExample Replacement
AttentionAdult or peer attentionTeach a way to request attention appropriately
EscapeRemoval from a non-preferred task or settingTeach a break request; reduce task demand temporarily
AccessA preferred item or activityTeach a waiting or requesting strategy
SensoryInternal regulation (calm, stimulation)Provide sensory tools or movement breaks

Components of a BIP

  1. Target behavior — defined specifically and observably ("calls out" not "disruptive")
  2. Function — identified from the FBA
  3. Replacement behavior — serves the same function, appropriately
  4. Antecedent strategies — changes to the environment that reduce the need for the behavior
  5. Teaching plan — how the replacement is taught, by whom, how often
  6. Reinforcement plan — what happens when the student uses the replacement behavior
  7. Response plan — what staff do when the problem behavior occurs
  8. Crisis plan (if needed) — safety procedures
  9. Data collection — who tracks what, how often
  10. Review schedule — when the team meets to adjust

Roles on the BIP Team

  • School psychologist — leads FBA, co-writes BIP, trains staff
  • Case manager — ensures BIP aligns with IEP, manages paperwork
  • Classroom teachers — implement the plan consistently, collect data
  • Paraprofessionals — support implementation per their role in the plan
  • Administrator — handles discipline decisions, ensures legal compliance
  • Family — included in plan development, kept informed of progress
  • Counselor — coordinates, supports student, may deliver social skills instruction

Monitoring Success

  • Target behavior decreases in frequency, intensity, or duration
  • Replacement behavior is used consistently by the student
  • Data collection is happening and being reviewed regularly (every 2–4 weeks)
  • All staff are following the plan consistently — fidelity checks happen
